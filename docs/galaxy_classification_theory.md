## Historical Background

It was in the middle of the eighteenth century that Kant and Wright first suggested that the Milky Way represents a finite-sized disk-like system of stars. As an extension of their philosophical argument about the nature of the Galaxy, Kant went on to suggest that if the Milky Way is limited in extent, perhaps the diffuse and very faint "elliptical nebulae" seen in the night sky might actually be extremely distant disk-like systems, similar to our own. He called these objects **island universes**.

### The Hubble Classification System

As a first step in understanding any new collection of objects, it is necessary to classify them according to their intrinsic characteristics. Hubble played a major role in this classification. In his book "The realm of nebulae", Hubble proposed that galaxies be grouped into three primary categories based on their overall appearance.

This morphological classification scheme, known as the **Hubble sequence**, divides galaxies into:
- **Ellipticals (E's)**
- **Spirals**
- **Irregulars (Irr's)**

The spirals are further subdivided into two parallel sequences:
- **Normal spirals (S's)**
- **Barred spirals (SB's)**

A transitional class of galaxies between ellipticals and spirals is known as **lenticulars**. It can be either:
- **Normal (S0's)**
- **Barred (SB0's)**

Hubble then arranged this morphological sequence in the form of a **tuning-fork diagram**.

![Hubble Tuning Fork Diagram](https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/72499426/98267342-48717680-1fb1-11eb-8a40-2df56a47a643.png)

### Early vs. Late Type Terminology

Hubble originally thought (incorrectly) that the tuning-fork diagram could be interpreted as an evolutionary sequence for galaxies. As a result, he referred to galaxies toward the left of the diagram as **early types** and to those toward the right as **late types**, terminology that is still in widespread use today.

## The Hubble Sequence

### Ellipticity Definition

Within the category of ellipticals, Hubble made divisions based on the observed ellipticity of the galaxy, defined by:

```
ε ≡ 1 - α/β
```

Where:
- **α** = apparent minor axis of the ellipse
- **β** = apparent major axis of the ellipse
- Both projected onto the plane of the sky

**Important note**: Galaxies with ellipticities greater than ε=0.7 have never been observed, implying that no E galaxies with intrinsic ellipticities greater than 0.7 appear to exist. The apparent ellipticity may not correspond well to an actual ellipticity since the orientation of the spheroid to our line of sight plays a crucial role in our observations.

![Ellipticity Diagram](https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/72499426/98267594-91c1c600-1fb1-11eb-9828-e0a5cf961d0b.png)

### 3D Ellipsoidal Shapes

**Oblate Spheroidal Galaxy**:
- Axis lengths: a = b and c < a

![Oblate Spheroid](https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/72499426/98268215-42c86080-1fb2-11eb-9417-1b94a351b6d3.png)

**Prolate Spheroidal Galaxy**:
- Axis lengths: b = c and a > b

**Triaxial Ellipsoid**:
- Axis lengths: a > b > c

### Spiral Subdivisions

Hubble subdivided the spiral sequences into:
- **Normal spirals**: Sa, Sab, Sb, Sbc, Sc
- **Barred spirals**: SBa, SBab, SBb, SBbc, SBc

**Characteristics by subtype**:

| Type | Bulge Size | Bulge/Disk Ratio | Spiral Arms | Star Distribution |
|------|-----------|------------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Sa/SBa** | Most prominent | L_bulge/L_disk ~ 0.3 | Tightly wound | Smoothest |
| **Sc/SBc** | Smaller | L_bulge/L_disk ~ 0.005 | Loose | More clumpy |

### Irregular Galaxies

Hubble split irregulars into:
- **Irr I**: At least some hint of organized structure (e.g., spiral arms)
- **Irr II**: Most extremely disorganized structures

### Lenticular Refinements

Lenticular galaxies are also sometimes subdivided according to dust absorption in their disks:
- **S01/SB01**: No dust in disks
- **S03/SB03**: Significant amounts of dust

### De Vaucouleurs Refinement

To make finer distinctions between normal and barred spirals, de Vaucouleurs suggested:
- **SA**: Normal spirals (instead of simply S)
- **SAB**: Intermediate types with weak bars
- **SB**: Strongly barred galaxies

## Galaxy Types and Properties

Galaxies are basically of three types: **Elliptical**, **Spiral**, and **Irregular**.

### 1. Elliptical Galaxies (E Galaxy / Early Type)

#### Classification
They have smooth light distributions. The Hubble classifications of these galaxies contain an integer 'n' that describes the elongation of the galaxy image. The classification is determined by the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the galaxy's **isophotes** (a line in a diagram connecting points where the intensity of the light is the same).

**Formula**:
```
n = 10(1 - b/a)
```

Where:
- **b** = semi-minor axis
- **a** = semi-major axis
- **n** ranges from 0 to 7

**Examples**:
- **E0 galaxy**: Looks like a circle
- **E7 galaxy**: Looks long and elongated

#### 3D Structure
In a 3D ellipsoidal shape with semi-axes a, b, c:
- **a = b > c**: Oblate
- **a > b = c**: Prolate
- **a > b > c**: Triaxial

#### Physical Properties
- ❌ **No current star formation**
- 🌌 Usually found in **galaxy clusters and compact groups**
- ⭐ Contain **old stars**
- 💨 Little **gas and dust**

**M89 - E0 Galaxy**

### 2. Spiral Galaxies (S Galaxy / Late Type)

#### Components
- Spiral arms
- Bulge
- Bar (in barred spirals)
- Spheroid

#### Physical Properties
- ✨ Light profiles with **characteristic spiral spin**
- ⭐ Contain both **new and old stars**
- 🌟 **New stars formed** especially in spiral arms
- 🌌 Seem to **avoid dense groups**
- 📊 Very **rare in clusters**

### 3. Irregular Galaxies (Irr Galaxy)

#### Characteristics
- 🔄 **No distinct regular shape** like spiral or elliptical galaxies
- ❌ **Do not fall into any classes** of Hubble sequence
- 🌌 Usually found in **groups or clusters** where collisions or near-misses between galaxies are common

#### Starburst Galaxies
One type of irregular galaxy is the **Starburst Galaxy**:
- 💫 Shine very brightly
- ⭐ New stars are born in a **short period of time**

#### Example

**NGC 1427A - Irregular Galaxy**

![NGC 1427A](https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/72499426/98271879-6ab9c300-1fb6-11eb-9e51-9cc1b8d2ece8.jpg)

### 4. Lenticular Galaxies (Armless Spiral / S0)

#### Characteristics
- 🔵 Have **central bulge** but **no spiral arms**
- 🔍 If the central bulge is not very bright, difficult to differentiate from **E0 galaxy**

#### Subtypes
- **S0**: Normal lenticular galaxies
- **SB0**: Barred lenticular galaxies (have bars like spiral galaxies)

#### Example

## Applications in Modern Astronomy

### Galaxy Zoo Project

Most galaxies can be broadly separated into two morphological types: **spiral** and **elliptical**.

**Galaxy Zoo** was the first attempt to analyze the distribution of a large number of spiral and elliptical galaxies in the local universe. Using the power of **crowd sourcing**, it provided morphological classifications of nearly **900,000 galaxies**.

#### Recent Developments

A more recent catalog of galaxy morphology is the catalog of **~3,000,000 Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS)** galaxies classified automatically using **machine learning**.

**Limitation**: While the catalog is large, the vast majority of the galaxies in that catalog do not have spectroscopic data.

#### Modern Automated Classification

The automated catalog provides:
- **~1.5 million galaxies** with 98% agreement rate with Galaxy Zoo 1 debiased "super clean" accuracy
- Publicly available at: https://figshare.com/articles/Morphology_and_photometric_redshift_catalog/4833593

**Note**: Like Galaxy Zoo, it represents the galaxies in the catalog, and not necessarily a complete and unbiased sample of SDSS galaxies.

### Photometric Redshifts

An area of astrophysics that has greatly increased in popularity in the last few years is the estimation of redshifts from **photometric data**.

#### Why Photometric Redshifts?

Although the distances are less accurate than those obtained with spectra, the **sheer number of objects** with photometric measurements can often make up for the reduction in individual accuracy by suppressing the statistical noise of an ensemble calculation.

#### For Galaxies

At low redshifts, the calculation of photometric redshifts for normal galaxies is quite straightforward due to the **break in the typical galaxy spectrum at 4000Å**.

As a galaxy is redshifted with increasing distance, the color (measured as a difference in magnitudes) changes relatively smoothly.

**Performance**:
- Both template and empirical photo-z approaches obtain similar results
- Root-mean-square deviation of **~0.02 in redshift**
- Close to the best possible result given the intrinsic spread in properties

#### For Quasars/AGN

Historically, the calculation of photometric redshifts for quasars and other AGN has been even more difficult than for galaxies, because:

- 📊 Spectra are dominated by **bright but narrow emission lines**
- 🎨 In broad photometric pass bands, emission lines can dominate the color
- ⚠️ Subject to several effects:
  - **Degeneracy**: One emission line appearing like another at a different redshift
  - **Disappearance**: An emission line disappearing between survey filters
  - **Reddening**: Color distortion from dust

The assignment of quasar photo-z is thus a complex problem amenable to **data mining** in a similar manner to the classification of AGN.

---

### Spectral Classification

Many of the physical properties, and thus classification, of a galaxy are determined by its **stellar population**. The spectrum of a galaxy is therefore another method for classification and can sometimes produce a clearer link to the underlying physics than the morphology.

**Importance**: Spectral classification is important because it is possible for a range of morphological types to have the same spectral type, and vice versa, because spectral types are driven by different underlying physical processes.

---

### Galaxy Zoo Crowd-Sourcing Approach

Recently, the popular Galaxy Zoo project has taken an alternative approach to morphological classification, employing **crowd sourcing**:

#### How It Works
- An application was made available online
- Members of the general public viewed images from the SDSS
- They assigned classifications according to an outlined scheme

#### Success Metrics
- Over **100,000 people** participated
- Provided over **40 million classifications** in six months
- Sample of **893,212 galaxies**, mostly to limiting depth of r = 17.77 mag

#### Categories
Classifications included categories not previously assigned in astronomical data mining studies:
- Edge-on orientation
- Handedness of spiral arms
- Multiple morphological features

#### Comparison with Automated Methods

The approach represents a **complementary** one to automated algorithms:

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| **Human Classification** | Can see things algorithms miss, different systematic errors | Runtime: 6 months for 40M classifications |
| **Automated (ANN)** | Same 40M classifications in a few minutes | May miss subtle features humans detect |

## Challenges in Classification

### General Challenges

#### 1. Statistical Inference with Very Large-N Datasets

- Statistical inference and visualization with very-large-N datasets represents a **scientific and technological framework** needed to cope with this data flood
- Considerable work is being conducted by computer scientists and applied mathematicians in other applied fields

**Key Issues**:
- Computational challenges with large numbers of data vectors
- Large dimensionality
- Highly **non-trivial statistical problems**
- Driven by the **heterogeneity and intrinsic complexity** of the data

#### 2. Multivariate Analysis with Measurement Errors and Censoring

Astrophysicists often devote as much effort to **precise determination of their errors** as they devote to the measurements:
- Instruments carefully calibrated to reduce systematic uncertainties
- Background levels and random fluctuations carefully evaluated
- Determination of random errors

#### 3. Probability and Bayesian Computation

- **Bayes Theorem** and **Bayes factors** are becoming increasingly well known in astronomical research
- **Challenge**: Conceptual - astronomers need training in how to construct likelihoods for familiar parametric situations

#### 4. Links Between Astrophysical Theory and Wavelets

Wavelet analysis suffers a profound limitation in astronomy - theoretical challenges in connecting wavelet decompositions to physical models.

---

### Theoretical Challenges

#### 1. Surveys of Galaxy Redshifts

Application of the **redshift-distance relation** (Hubble's law) allows:
- Analysis of the **large-scale distribution of galaxies**
- Mapping of the **gravitational field**
- Understanding the **underlying density distribution**

**Critical Issues**:
- Estimation of many inherent **selection biases**
- Understanding **instrumental limitations**
- How our view of the universe is affected by our observational perspective
- How information is received by current technologies

#### 2. Analysis of Patterns in Galaxy Clustering

The current state of play regarding **correlation function analysis**:
- **Computational and sampling problems**
- **Fundamental limitations** in giving a complete statistical description of the pattern
- Various techniques devised to quantify the **topology of the clustering pattern** with varying degrees of success

#### 3. Assessment of Sub-clustering in Clusters of Galaxies

**Challenges**:
- Lack of adequate cluster samples to provide an **unbiased look** at the problem
- Lack of supplemental data in most clusters to **confirm or reject** possible small-scale structures

#### 4. High-Redshift Galaxy Studies

- **Reionization Sources**
- **Lyman Alpha Emitters**
- **Dropout Galaxies**
- **Luminosity Function**

---

### Challenges in Data-Rich Astronomy

#### 1. Star-Galaxy Image Classification

A classical problem in the analysis of astronomical panoramic imagery. The characteristic resolution of an astronomical image is given through a combination of the instrumental resolution.

#### 2. Dynamical, Real-Time Classification of Astronomical Transient Events

A need for dynamical, real-time scientific measurement systems, consisting of:
- **Discovery instruments or sensors**
- **Real-time computational analysis and decision engine**
- **Optimized follow-up instruments** which can be deployed selectively in (or near) real-time
- **Feedback loop**: Measurements feed back into the analysis immediately
